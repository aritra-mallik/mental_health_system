The article explains that panic attacks and anxiety attacks are often confused but are actually different in how they start, feel, and last. Panic attacks occur suddenly, often without a clear trigger, and involve intense symptoms like chest pain, rapid heartbeat, and fear of dying. Postpartum depression is a serious form of depression that can occur after childbirth, going beyond the short-term “baby blues” that many new mothers experience. It involves more intense and longer-lasting symptoms such as severe mood swings, excessive crying, fatigue, anxiety, and difficulty bonding with the baby. These symptoms can interfere with daily life and the ability to care for the child, and may begin during pregnancy or within a year after birth. Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) is a long-lasting form of depression that may be less intense than major depression but continues for years.
Its key feature is a chronic low or sad mood, along with symptoms like low energy, poor concentration, sleep problems, and low self-esteem.
Unlike major depression, the duration of symptoms—typically two years or more—is more important than their severity for diagnosis.
The condition often begins early in life and may feel like a normal part of a person’s personality, which can prevent them from seeking help.
Treatment usually involves a combination of psychotherapy and medication, which can reduce symptoms and improve quality of life over time.

Anhedonia is the inability to feel pleasure or interest in activities that once brought enjoyment, and it is a common symptom of depression and other mental health conditions.
It can affect different areas of life, including social interactions (social anhedonia) and physical experiences like eating or touch (physical anhedonia).
People with anhedonia may feel emotionally numb, lose motivation, withdraw from activities or relationships, and find it hard to look forward to things.
The exact cause is not fully understood, but it is linked to changes in brain reward systems, particularly involving dopamine and related neural pathways.
Treatment focuses on addressing the underlying condition and may include therapy, medications, and lifestyle changes to help restore a sense of pleasure and improve overall well-being.

Decision fatigue is a state of mental exhaustion that occurs after making too many decisions, reducing a person’s ability to make good choices.
It is caused by the number, complexity, and stress level of decisions a person faces throughout the day, with harder decisions draining energy more quickly.
Common signs include tiredness, brain fog, and a growing sense of mental strain as the day progresses.

Managing decision fatigue involves reducing unnecessary decisions, simplifying choices, and preserving mental energy to improve decision-making quality.

Autonomous sensory meridian response (ASMR) is a calming sensation—often described as a tingling feeling on the scalp and neck—triggered by gentle sounds or visual stimuli like whispering or slow movements.
Many people use ASMR as a sleep aid because it promotes deep relaxation, reduces stress, and helps quiet the mind before bedtime.
It may also improve sleep quality by increasing positive brain chemicals like dopamine and oxytocin, while lowering anxiety and emotional tension. Common triggers include soft voices, tapping, brushing sounds, or personal attention actions such as hair stroking or gentle touch.

The stop-thought (or thought-stopping) technique is a cognitive-behavioral strategy used to interrupt unwanted or negative thoughts by consciously telling oneself to “stop.”
It often involves using cues like saying “stop” out loud or mentally, snapping a rubber band, or visualizing a stop sign to break the thought pattern.
The goal is to reduce anxiety, intrusive thoughts, or negative thinking by replacing them with more positive or neutral thoughts.
However, modern psychology suggests that this technique may not always be effective and can sometimes make unwanted thoughts return even stronger.
Because of this, many experts now recommend alternative approaches like mindfulness or acceptance-based strategies to manage intrusive thoughts more effectively.

https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=xed8CAghZiU&list=RDxed8CAghZiU&start_radio=1

Attachment styles are patterns of how people form emotional bonds and relationships, shaped mainly by early experiences with caregivers.
There are four main types: secure, stressed (preoccupied), avoidant (dismissive), and disorganized (fearful), each reflecting different levels of trust, intimacy, and emotional regulation.
Secure attachment is the healthiest, where individuals feel comfortable with closeness and independence and can maintain stable relationships.
Insecure styles include stressed (fear of abandonment and need for reassurance), avoidant (emotional distance and independence), and disorganized (a mix of fear and desire for closeness).
These attachment patterns can influence adult relationships, but they are not fixed and can change over time with awareness and effort.
